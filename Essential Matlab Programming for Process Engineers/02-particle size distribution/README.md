# Working with Particle Size Distributions

## Topic

This lecture introduces a complete data-processing workflow using real particle size distribution (PSD) data from a laser-diffraction measurement. Starting from a raw CSV file, students compute, visualise, and export a fully annotated result — covering numerical integration, interpolation, struct variables, and the JSON data format along the way.

## Physical Background

A **particle size distribution** describes what fraction of the total particle volume lies in each size class. Two representations are used:

- **Density distribution** `q3(x)` — volume fraction per unit size interval [1/µm]. The subscript 3 denotes volume-weighting (as opposed to number- or area-weighting).
- **Cumulative distribution** `Q3(x)` — total volume fraction of particles *smaller than* `x`. By definition `Q3(∞) = 1`.

---

## Equations

### 1 — Bin geometry and coordinate conventions

The measurement instrument divides the size axis into bins of equal width $\Delta x$.
Each bin $i$ spans from $x_{i} - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}$ to $x_{i} + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}$.

The two quantities are associated with **different x-coordinates**:

| Quantity | Symbol | Plotted over | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Density distribution | $q_3(x_i)$ | **bin centre** $x_i$ | $q_3$ is the average volume fraction per unit length *within* the bin; it is a local property of the interval |
| Cumulative distribution | $Q_3(x_{\text{upper},i})$ | **bin upper edge** $x_{\text{upper},i} = x_i + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}$ | $Q_3(x)$ is defined as the total volume fraction of particles *smaller than x*; after summing through bin $i$, all particles up to the upper edge of that bin have been counted |

In the script this is reflected by:

```matlab
psd.x       % bin centres  – used for q3
psd.x_upper = psd.x + 0.5*psd.dx   % upper edges – used for Q3 and d-values
```

### 2 — Density distribution $q_3$

$q_3(x)$ is the **volume-weighted density distribution** (ISO 9276-2, subscript $r=3$):

$$q_3(x) = \frac{1}{V_\text{total}}\frac{dV}{dx}$$

where $dV$ is the volume of particles with diameters in $[x,\, x+dx]$.
Its unit is 1/length (here: 1/µm), and it satisfies:

$$\int_0^\infty q_3(x)\, dx = 1$$

The analogous number-weighted and area-weighted distributions $q_0$ and $q_2$ have the same form with volume replaced by particle count or surface area, respectively.

### 3 — Cumulative distribution $Q_3$ by numerical integration

$Q_3(x)$ is the fraction of the total volume consisting of particles *smaller than* $x$:

$$Q_3(x) = \int_0^{x} q_3(x')\, dx'$$

Because $q_3$ is given at bin centres and assumed constant within each bin, the integral from 0 to the upper edge of bin $i$ is approximated by the rectangle rule:

$$Q_3(x_{\text{upper},i}) = \sum_{j=1}^{i} q_3(x_j)\cdot \Delta x$$

This is exactly what `cumsum(psd.q3 * psd.dx)` computes.
The rectangle rule is valid here because $\Delta x$ is constant — for non-uniform bin widths a more careful approach (e.g. `cumtrapz`) would be needed.

The result is normalised to 1 to compensate for discretisation and truncation errors at the distribution tails:

$$Q_3 \leftarrow \frac{Q_3}{\max(Q_3)}$$

### 4 — Characteristic diameters by inverse interpolation

The diameter $d_n$ is the size at which exactly $n\%$ of the total volume consists of smaller particles, i.e. the solution to $Q_3(d_n) = n/100$:

$$d_n = Q_3^{-1}\!\left(\frac{n}{100}\right)$$

Because $Q_3$ is a monotonically increasing, tabulated function, this inversion is done numerically via `interp1` — with the roles of x and y **swapped** compared to a normal lookup:

```matlab
psd.d50 = interp1(psd.Q3, psd.x_upper, 0.5)
%                 ^^^^^^^^ these are the y-values (what we know)
%                           ^^^^^^^^^^^^^ these are the x-values (what we want)
```

Computed for $n \in \{10,\,16,\,50,\,84,\,90\}$.

### 5 — Geometric standard deviation

For a log-normal distribution, the shape is fully described by the median $d_{50}$ and the geometric standard deviation $\sigma_g$. The latter can be estimated from symmetric percentile pairs around the median:

$$\sigma_{g,1} = \frac{d_{50}}{d_{16}}, \qquad \sigma_{g,2} = \frac{d_{84}}{d_{50}}$$

For a perfectly log-normal distribution $\sigma_{g,1} = \sigma_{g,2}$. A difference between the two indicates skewness of the distribution.

### 6 — Span

The span is a dimensionless measure of the width of the distribution, normalised by the median:

$$\text{Span} = \frac{d_{90} - d_{10}}{d_{50}}$$

A narrow (monodisperse) distribution has Span $\ll 1$; a broad polydisperse distribution has Span $\gg 1$.

## Learning Goals

After completing this lecture, students should be able to:

1. **Import structured measurement data** from a CSV file with comment headers using `readmatrix`.
2. **Organise data in MATLAB structs** instead of isolated variables, and explain why structs are preferable for multi-field measurement data.
3. **Compute a cumulative distribution** from a density distribution by numerical integration with `cumsum`, and explain when the rectangle rule is a valid approximation.
4. **Extract quantiles by inverse interpolation** using `interp1` with x and y arguments swapped.
5. **Interpret PSD statistics**: explain what $d_{50}$, $\sigma_g$, and span mean physically, and how they relate to process parameters in powder production.
6. **Create a dual-axis plot** with `yyaxis` and annotate characteristic values with `xline`.
7. **Export results as JSON** and explain why a self-documenting, human-readable format is better practice than saving raw `.mat` files when data needs to be shared with colleagues or other tools.
8. **Read back a JSON file** and verify that the round-trip (MATLAB struct → JSON → MATLAB struct) preserves all values.

## Notes on Units

The raw CSV stores `x` in **metres**; the script converts to **micrometres** at import so that the unit of `q3` (1/µm) and the unit of `dx` (µm) are consistent. Always check unit consistency before integrating — the normalisation step masks unit errors but does not fix them.
